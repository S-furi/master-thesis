\chapter{Reactivity and Dependency Management}\label{ch:reactvity_dep_mgmnt}

The previous chapter introduced what is a simulator, with an emphasis to
the discrete-event formalism. Moreover, the key concept of dependency management
is introduced in such systems with emphasis on complex systems with sparse
dependencies such as biochemical systems kinetic. This chapter aims to introduce
a complementary viewpoint in dependency management and resolution through
\emph{reactivity} as a general-purpose technique for expressing and executing
such dependency structures.

Reactive programming is often present as a software-engineering style for
building responsive user interfaces or streaming
applications~\cite{kuhn2017RDP}, but at more abstract level, however, it
provides a vocabulary and a set of execution strategies and principles for
maintaining \emph{derived values} and \emph{event-driven processes} consistent
with a changing base state (e.g. a simulated environment that changes over
time). In other words, it can be interpreted as a dependency resolution
mechanism: changes propagate through a set of dependencies in an implicit,
dynamic graph triggering recomputation, notifications and potentially the
scheduling of future work.

In what follows, an extensive theoretical foundation is built around three
complementary formalisms:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \emph{event-riven} and \emph{data-flow} models, which view
	      computation as the propagation of tokens or events through a network of
	      operations;
	\item the \emph{observer} family of patterns, traditionally introduced in
	      object-oriented design, which popularised callback-based push
	      propagation and decoupled notification;
	\item \emph{Functional Reactive Programming}, which provides specific
	      meanings and manipulation tools to time-varying values and event
	      streams.
\end{itemize}
We then connect these ideas to modern reactive frameworks (with Project Reactor
as a concrete reference point) and highlight the implications for designing
reactive dependency management in a discrete-event simulation engine.

\section{Theory of Reactive Systems}

\subsection{Event-driven and Data-flow Computation}

A natural conceptual root of reactive programming is \emph{event-driven}
computation. In such systems, execution follows a data-driven model, where each
instruction becomes executable only once all required operands have been
preceding instructions~\cite{dennis1974PABDFP}. Applied to software systems,
such formalism define a system organised as a graph of components whose
dependencies are realised through the flow of messages. Systems developed under
such assumptions are usually employed to exploit parallelism and concurrency,
especially for distributed systems, which introduces a higher degree of
complexity due to how communication is handled among the nodes of the system.
Typical event-driven applications rely on \emph{asynchronous communication},
which avoids keeping nodes in hold when sending messages while using explicit
mechanism of synchronisation when needed (\Cref{ssec:sync_async}). A perfect
exemplification of such architecture at the programming-language level, is the
\emph{actor model} which is mainly based on message-passing. In the actor model
each actor encapsulates state and behaviour, processes one message at a time,
and interacts exclusively by sending messages to other actors. This yields a
compositional model where concurrency emerges from independent actors
\emph{reacting} to incoming
messages~\cite{agha1986AMCCDS}.

\subsubsection{Data-flow}
A complementary tradition is \emph{data-flow} computation, where programs are
expressed as networks of operators and edges represent the flow of values.
Data-flow is especially relevant to reactivity because it makes dependencies
\emph{explicit}: a node can run when its inputs are available and the graph
structure provides a concrete representation of what depends on what.

In \ac{SDF} production and consumption rates are fixed, enabling static
scheduling and bounded buffer analysis~\cite{lee1987SDF}. Beyond \ac{SDF} as a
graph model, some languages were designed around the data-flow as a programming
abstraction. \textsc{Lucid} treats variables as time-indexed streams and
expresses programs as systems of equations over those
streams~\cite{wadge1985LUCID}. Moreover, \textsc{Lustre} targets reactive and
embedded control systems, combining a synchronous interpretation of time with a
data-flow notation that supports analysis and compilation to efficient
sequential code~\cite{halbwachs1991LUSTRE}.

In this thesis these language are not implementation targets, instead we
extract a conceptual lesson: reactivity can be specified \emph{declaratively}
as relationships over time-varying quantities and the runtime/compiler can
exploit the explicit dependency structure for scheduling and optimisations.

\subsection{Reactive systems}
The term \emph{reactive systems} was originally coined to distinguish systems
that  maintain a continuous interaction with their environment from
transformational systems that simply compute a result from
inputs~\cite{harel1989DRS}. In modern software engineering however, this notion
denotes systems that must continuously respond to incoming stimuli (user
actions, network messages, sensor readings) under certain constraints of
latency and availability. The \emph{Reactive Manifesto}\footnote{
	\url{https://reactivemanifesto.org/}} organises this space under four
high-level properties: responsiveness, resilience, elasticity and
message-driven interaction.

Although the manifesto is not a formal model of computation, it highlights
constraints that directly affect dependency resolution mechanisms:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \emph{backpressure} and load regulation, i.e. how the system
	      constraints propagation rates under load;
	\item \emph{asynchrony} and distribution, which improves responsiveness but
	      make ordering and consistency explicit concerns;
	\item \emph{failure} as a first-class concern, which motivates clear or
	      explicit lifecycle management for subscription and resources.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Observer pattern and push-based propagation}
In object-oriented systems, reactivity is traditionally implemented through the
\emph{Observer pattern}~\cite{gamma1994design}. A \emph{subject} maintains a
set of \emph{observers} and notifies them whenever its state changes.
Therefore, the pattern decouples producers from consumers: the subject does not
need to know what observers do with the updates, only that they implement a
common notification interface.

From a dependency-management viewpoint, observer-style reactivity correspond to
a \emph{push-based} propagation, where updates originate at a source which
\emph{immediately} (\emph{synchronous}) push them to dependents which may in
turn update their own status.

This approach is simple and effective, but it introduces well-known challenges
to the point that some may argue to ``Deprecate the Observer pattern''~\cite{maier2010DOP}.
Among some notable issues we find
\begin{enumerate*}[label=(\roman*)]
	\item the dependency graph is often implicit in registrations spread across
	      the codebase
	\item ordering constraints are subtle when several observers depend on one
	      another
	\item feedback cycles and re-entrant notifications can create inconsistent
	      intermediate state (link to glitches) and
	\item lifecycle management becomes critical (link to section)
\end{enumerate*}

These issues motivate richer reactive abstractions where dependencies become
explicit and where the runtime can provide stronger guarantees about lifecycles
and propagation order, along with a set of reactive abstractions that improve
composability.

\subsection{Functional Reactive Programming}
\ac{FRP} emerged as an attempt to provide a more principled account of reactive
computation. In the classical \ac{FRP} formulation, time-varying values are
treated as first-class values and discrete occurrences are represented as
\emph{event streams}~\cite{elliott1997FRA}. More specifically, the two most
common reactive entities are the algebraic types of \emph{behaviours} (also
referred to as \emph{signals}) which represents values that varies over time
and \emph{events} which represent discrete occurrences that happen at
particular instants. Originally, a behaviour was modelled as function of time
(continuous-time), while an event is a time-ordered collection of occurrences.
However, \ac{FRP} has later been popularised and expanded by
\cite{hudak2000PFRP} as a form of general \emph{framework} for hybrid systems
programming, where continuous evolution and event-driven changes coexist. A
central benefit of this viewpoint is that programs can be expressed
\emph{declaratively}, where instead of manually maintaining state and
registering callbacks (e.g. as in the observer pattern), one defines behaviours
and events by composing \emph{primitives} and \emph{combinators}, and the
runtime provides an operational interpretation. Moreover, further works as
\cite{zhanyong2000FRP} shows even at a higher detail how continuous time could
be derived from continuous sampling of event-streams, demonstrating that even
some intuitive \ac{FRP} primitives impose non-trivial constraints if one wants
to preserve the intended semantics. This analysis is important because it
clarifies that \ac{FRP} is not merely a set of APIs, but a semantic model whose
implementation choices affect both expressiveness and correctness.

In practice, \ac{FRP} systems differ along two major design axis:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \emph{continuous} or \emph{discrete}: whether time is treated as a
	      continuous domain (i.e. signals as mathematical functions) or primarily
	      as a sequence of discrete occurrences (streams);
	\item \emph{push} or \emph{pull} evaluation: whether changes are propagated
	      eagerly from sources or values are recomputed (lazily) on demand.
\end{itemize}
Modern mainstream approaches typically concentrate on the pair discrete-stream,
employing data streams and operators (e.g. \texttt{flatMap}, \texttt{filter},
\texttt{collect}) together with hybrid approaches when it comes to eager or
lazy data evaluation.

\section{Mechanics of Propagation}
The theoretical models in the previous section agree on a common operational
structure: a reactive application maintains some base state and a collection of
derived computations whose results must remain consistent with that base. A
non-trivial aspect of the design space of reactivity is about
\emph{propagation}, and more specifically what counts as an update, when it is
delivered, in which order and under which flow-control and with which lifecycle
rules.

\subsection{Synchronous vs. Asynchronous Propagation}\label{ssec:sync_async}

In a \emph{synchronous} propagation model, a state change triggers immediate
notification and recomputation in the \emph{same control flow} as the update.
This is common in in-process observer setups an many UI frameworks. While it is
very easy to reason about local synchronous communication, this approach can
lead to deep call chains, with re-entrancy hazard and most of the times with
unpredictable latency when a single update fans out to many dependencies.

On the other hand, in an \emph{asynchronous} model updates are decoupled from
their handles, effectively enqueuing work (either modeled through messages,
tasks or events) that is later executed by an event loop or a scheduler. This
is the dominant style in message-driven systems and actor-based architecture
~\cite{agha1986AMCCDS}. Asynchrony improves composability and isolation, but it
introduces ordering issues and thus requiring a stable notion of time
(especially true for distributed asynchronous communication) and
synchronisation mechanisms~\cite{lamport1978TCOEDS}.

While asynchronous execution and communication is very effective and the
de-facto standard in event-driven and reactive architecture, some domains may
require blocking communications in order to enforce strict ordering avoiding
clock synchronisation protocols (this is also applicable to
distributed/asynchronous networks~\cite{awerbuch1985CNS}). If we consider
\ac{DES}, an asynchronous event scheduling mechanism could be very inefficient
and resource wasteful, because there are only \emph{eventual} consistency
guarantees (both message arrival and ordering), therefore all the problems
discussed about speculative execution discussed in \Cref{ssec:speculative_pdes}
applies here. \cite{arjomandi1983ESVADS} demonstrates that in certain scenarios
synchronous communication is provably more efficient than its asynchronous
counterpart, suggesting that the design choice of the communication model is
very related to the problem at hand and will impact both the efficiency and the
cognitive load imposed to the designer and the programmer.

\subsection{Push vs. Pull updates}\label{ssec:push_pull}
Propagation can be further classified by whether data moves forward from
sources or is \emph{requested}. More in particular, we talk about
\emph{push-based} propagation if producers deliver updates to consumers
(observer-style notifications), while a \emph{pull-based} propagation model
sees consumers request values when needed, causing \emph{upstream}
recomputation.

Push-based notifications improves timeliness (immediate reaction), however can
waste work if updates are produced faster than consumers can process. Pull
reduces redundant work by making recomputation conditional on demand, but it
requires a policy for cache invalidation and for ensuring that dependent
computations observe a \emph{consistent} snapshot of inputs. Moreover, most
functional programming implementations used sampling (i.e. pulling values)
because it better fits the continuous-time semantics well, but is wasteful
because recomputes even when inputs haven't changed, also introducing latency
determined by the sample tick, namely the time gap from the occurrence of an
event and its reaction's effects. While both these problems are avoided in a
standard data-driven (push) system, the imperative nature of the programs and
the difficulty to compose for continuous-time behaviours make a designer chose
for alternative systems. Some hybrid approaches like \emph{push-pull}
\ac{FRP}~\cite{elliott2009PPFRP} aim to achieve the ``best of both worlds'' by
recomputing values only when input change (push), while maintaining the simple,
composable semantics of continuous time (pull).

Generally, a designer may choose a pull-based model for parts of a reactive
system where sampling is done on event values that change continuously over
time, and this is especially try in environments capable of lazy evaluation in
order to minimise performance waste. On the other hand, a push-based evaluation
model fits well in systems that require instantaneous reactions.

\section{Challenges of Reactive Systems}
Reactive solutions introduce a number of recurring issues that become central
when the dependency graph is large and dynamic over time. In the following
sections some of these issues along with how some of the solutions presented
above tackles those problems.

\subsection{Ordering, consistency and glitches}
Once the dependency graph branches and re-joins, update ordering becomes
observable. A derived computation that depends on two inputs may observe a
mixed intermediate state if those inputs update at different times. This
phenomenon, depicted in \Cref{fig:glitch}, is referred to as a
\emph{glitch}~\cite{cooper2006EDDCBYVL}.
%

	intermediate value (a \emph{glitch}) observable during dependency graph
	propagation. At $t_2$, node $D$ updates before node $B$ has processed the
	change in $A$. Therefore $D$ calculates its values using a mix of new data
	($C=3$) and stale data ($B=2$). }{fig:glitch}
%
Glitches can only happen in languages employing a push-based evaluation model,
and generally cause wasteful recomputation or, worse, they can cause program
error and inconsistencies in the presence of side effects (which callback and
push-based execution models encourage).

Various solutions to this problem have been proposed, originally representing
dependencies in a DAG and performing a topological sorting. Other approaches
can tackle this problem by transactional batching where computations are
ordered in a task queue modeled as a set, and duplicated jobs gets re-inserted
in the task queue. While several glitch-avoidance approaches have been
successfully implemented in in single computers, distributed glitch avoidance
is much more complicated because of network failures, delays and lack of global
clock~\cite{bainomugisha2013SRP}, however current research is going towards
efficient approaches with low communication overhead by means, for example, of
\emph{Multiparty \ac{FRP}}~\cite{moriguchi2023DDSMFRP}.

A special kind of glitch is the presence of feedback loops, namely cyclic
dependencies among time-varying values which can be intentional or, most of the
times, accidental. Naive propagation in the presence of cycles can lead to
non-termination or unstable oscillations. Robust reactive models must detect
cycles and either reject them or provide explicit constructs that define the
intended semantics of the feedback.

\subsection{Lifecycle and Resource Management}
While glitches and ordering concern the \emph{correctness} of active
propagation, a separate class of challenges arises regarding the
\emph{duration} and \emph{termination} of reactive dependencies. In traditional
imperative programming, data lifespan is usually bounded by the scope of the
variable holding it. In reactive systems, however, the \emph{inversion of
	control} inherent in the observer pattern creates an \emph{inversion of
	reference} that complicates garbage collection and resource management.

\subsubsection{The Lapsed Listener Problem}
The most prominent manifestation of this issue is the \emph{Lapsed Listener
	Problem}, a term coined to describe memory leaks in observer-based systems.
Fundamentally, for a subject to notify an observer, it must hold a reference to
it. In many implementations (such as standard Java \texttt{Listener}s or C\#
\texttt{event}s), this is a \emph{strong reference}. This creates a
reachability graph where the \emph{source} keeps the \emph{sink} alive.

Consider a short-lived component (e.g., a simulation agent or a UI widget) that
subscribes to a long-lived global signal (e.g., a global clock or environment
state). Even when the short-lived component is logically ``destroyed'' or
removed from the scene, the global source retains a reference to it via the
callback. Consequently, the garbage collector cannot reclaim the component's
memory. Over time, these ``zombie'' listeners accumulate, leading to a
monotonic increase in memory usage, representing a classic memory
leak~\cite{maier2010DOP}.

\subsubsection{Unwanted Recomputation and Zombies}
Beyond memory pressure, lapsed listeners introduce computational overhead. A
``zombie'' node that remains attached to the graph continues to receive updates
and perform recomputations, even though its result is ignored by the rest of
the application.

In \ac{FRP} and data-flow graphs, this issue is subtle. If a derived signal $C$
depends on $A$ and $B$, but the application stops observing $C$, an eager
evaluation engine might continue updating $C$ whenever $A$ changes. To prevent
this, reactive runtimes must implement sophisticated \emph{dynamic graph
	pruning} or Reference Counting strategies to ensure that only signals with
active sinks (subscribers) are evaluated.

\subsubsection{Management Strategies}
Frameworks address these lifecycle challenges through three primary strategies:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \emph{Weak References:} The subject holds a weak reference to the
	      observer, allowing the GC to reclaim it if no other objects reference
	      it. While this solves the memory leak, it introduces non-deterministic
	      cleanup and does not necessarily stop the ``unwanted recomputation''
	      immediately, while also introducing some performance degradation issues
	      due to the additional indirection checks that weak collections must
	      perform on their elements;
	\item \emph{Explicit Disposal (The Subscription Object):} Modern reactive
	      implementation (like
	      \textsc{RxJava}\footnote{\url{https://github.com/ReactiveX/RxJava}},
	      Project Reactor\footnote{\url{https://projectreactor.io/}}) return a
	      \texttt{Disposable} or \texttt{Subscription} token upon registration.
	      The consumer is responsible for explicitly calling \texttt{dispose()}
	      to sever the link. This restores control to the consumer but
	      re-introduces the manual resource management that GC-languages aim to
	      eliminate.
	\item \emph{Ownership Transfer and Scopes:} Structured concurrency and
	      scoped reactivity (e.g., in Kotlin Coroutines or UI-bound lifecycles)
	      bind the subscription to a parent context. When the parent scope
	      terminates, all child subscriptions are automatically cancelled,
	      ensuring both memory safety and computational efficiency by
	      construction.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Performance considerations}
Reactive APIs or fully-reactive programming languages like
\textsc{Fran}~\cite{elliott1997FRA} are often advertised as declarative and
composable, but their runtime behaviour can be counter-intuitive because small
changes in operator structure, scheduling or allocation patterns can lead to
large differences in throughput and latency.

A first class of costs can be attributed to \emph{allocation} and
\emph{indirection overhead}. Operator pipelines are frequently implemented as
chains of small objects (subscribers, continuations, lambdas) that wrap one
another. If a pipeline is built and torn down repeatedly of if each event
allocates short-lived objects, garbage-collection pressure can dominate
execution. Practical runtimes therefore try to minimise per-event allocations
(e.g. by reusing buffers) and to apply \emph{operator fusion} to reduce
intermediate objects and call depth.

A second class of issues is caused by unbounded buffering for asynchronous
systems. Asynchrony is typically implemented via queues which (when lacking
flow-control mechanisms) bursts of data can create large backlogs causing
spikes in memory usage and latency due to queuing delays). This is the reason
behind the \emph{backpressure} mechanism (e.g. reactive streams in
\textsc{ReactiveX}) where the demand is made explicit so that the upstream
producers are forced to respect downstream capacity.

Finally, at a more lower level, dynamic-dependency graphs can harm locality. In
fact, if dependencies are frequently created and destroyed (e.g. short-lived
subscriptions, changing observers), caches and branch predictors have little
opportunity to stabilise~\cite{mukkara2017CGSECMLGP, mukkara2018ELGATHATS}.

\subsection{Distributed environments}

While reactive programming has successfully addressed the complexities of
dependency management in local applications, extending these guarantees to a
distributed setting remains an open challenge~\cite{bainomugisha2013SRP}. In a
single-host environment the runtime can assume a \emph{shared-memory} space and
a \emph{global clock}, allowing it to enforce consistency (glitch freedom) by,
for example, topologically sorting the dependency graph and processing updates
sequentially. However, in a distributed environment, the lack of a global
notion of time, the variable network latency and partial failures make such
naive propagation strategies insufficient~\cite{salvaneschi2013TDRP}.

\subsubsection{The Distributed Glitch Problem}
The primary issue in distributed reactivity is the preservation of consistency
across network boundaries. In local \ac{FRP}, a glitch is a temporary
inconsistency where a node observers a mix of old and new values during an
update propagation. On the other hand, in distributed settings this problem is
exacerbated because updates travelling through different network paths can
arrive out of order with significant delays, leading to ``spurious execution''
where the system reacts to an invalid intermediate
state~\cite{salvaneschi2013TDRP}.

\subsubsection{Consistency Trade-offs}
Achieving glitch freedom in a distributed setting imposes a heavy performance
penalty; this is especially true when enforcing a strict topological order
across nodes due to the need of distributed transactions or locking mechanism,
acting as a bottleneck reducing availability~\cite{margara2014DREAM}.
Consequently, distributed reactive frameworks often force developers to choose
between consistency and performance

Research suggests a taxonomy of propagation semantics for distributed
middleware:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \emph{Causal Consistency:} Ensures that updates that are causally
	      related are observed in the correct order. This is efficient and
	      minimises network traffic but does not prevent glitches (partial
	      updates).
	\item \emph{Glitch Freedom:} Guarantees that a node only recomputes when
	      \emph{all} relevant inputs for a specific logical time-step have
	      arrived. This requires the middleware to track the dependency graph and
	      buffer messages, increasing latency.
	\item \emph{Atomic Consistency:} Ensures a total ordering of updates across
	      the system, preventing concurrent propagation but incurring the highest
	      synchronisation cost.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Coupling vs. Resilience}
Finally, a fundamental tension exists between the explicit dependency
management of reactive programming and the resilience required by distributed
systems. Reactive programming typically relies on a tight coupling between
producers and consumers to maintain the dependency graph. However, distributed
systems usually favor loose coupling (e.g., Publish/Subscribe patterns) to
tolerate node failures and network partitions~\cite{bainomugisha2013SRP}.

Maintaining a precise dependency graph across a distributed system creates a
fragile architecture where a single node failure can halt propagation or leave
dependents in an undefined state. Consequently, the research landscape has
fragmented into various distinct paradigms to address this trade-off such as:
middleware approaches like \textsc{DREAM}~\cite{margara2014DREAM} which enforce
strict \emph{glitch freedom} through heavy coordination; industrial actor
frameworks like \textsc{Akka} which prioritise availability by accepting
\emph{nondeterminism} and \emph{eventual consistency} and recently
\textsc{Lingua Franca}~\cite{menard2023LF}, which utilises \emph{logical time}
to recover deterministic, glitch-free execution without the centralised
bottlenecks of traditional middleware.

\subsection{Industry Standards}

\subsubsection{RxJava (Reactive Extensions)}
RxJava represents the widespread adoption of the \emph{ReactiveX} API, which
combines the Observer pattern with the Iterator pattern and functional
programming paradigms. It employs a push-based propagation model where a source
(\texttt{Observable}) emits items to subscribers, offering an extensive
operator library for transforming, filtering, and combining asynchronous
streams. However, its design prioritises the handling of asynchronous data
pipelines over strict state consistency, providing no strict deterministic
ordering often necessitating complex external synchronisation to prevent race
conditions during state updates.

\subsubsection{Scala.React}
Emerging from the academic effort to deprecate the Observer pattern,
Scala.React provides a principled implementation of \ac{FRP} on the \ac{JVM}.
Unlike the manual callback management found in standard libraries, it
introduces first-class signals and events that automatically manage
dependencies. Its primary contribution is the enforcement of strong consistency
through algorithms that guarantee topological order during updates, effectively
eliminating glitches. Moreover it emphasises strong consistency and
composability taking inspiration from Reactor's core ideas.

\subsubsection{Akka}
Akka is the industry reference for building highly concurrent, distributed, and
resilient message-driven applications via the \emph{Actor Model}. Actors
encapsulate state and behavior, communicating exclusively through asynchronous
message passing, a model that excels at ``Location Transparency'' and
self-healing architectures. Despite these strengths, Akka exposes developers to
non-determinism and high-level race conditions~\cite{menard2023LF}. For a
software programs where reproducibility is paramount, relying on Akka's default
eventual consistency model often requires implementing complex virtual time
barriers to ensure that events are processed in the correct causal order.

\subsubsection{Kotlin Flows}
As a component of the Kotlin Coroutines library, Flows offer a modern
implementation of reactive streams deeply integrated with structured
concurrency. Flows operate on a ``cold'' execution model, meaning the reactive
pipeline is only active when collected. This approach leverages coroutines to
handle backpressure and asynchrony in a sequential, imperative style, avoiding
the operator complexity often associated with RxJava. While it offers superior
language integration, it remains targeted primarily at asynchronous data
processing rather than the precise, time-stepped propagation of updates
required by discrete-event software systems (e.g. a \ac{DES}).

\section{Summary}

This chapter established a theoretical framework for understanding reactivity
not merely as a UI pattern, but as a general-purpose mechanism for dependency
management and propagation. We validated that beyond its operational semantics,
reactive programming offers tangible software engineering benefits: empirical
studies demonstrate that reactive designs significantly enhance program
comprehension compared to traditional callback-based approaches like the
Observer pattern, particularly when managing complex data
flows~\cite{salvaneschi2017PERPSC}.

We first examined the foundations of \emph{event-driven} and \emph{data-flow}
computations, highlighting how explicit dependency graphs allow the runtime to
reason about scheduling and optimisation. We discussed how \ac{FRP} addresses
the ``inversion of reference'' issues (Lapsed Listeners) inherent in the
Observer pattern by elevating time-varying values (Behaviors) and discrete
occurrences (Events) to first-class citizens.

The mechanics of propagation were analysed along two critical axes:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \emph{Push vs. Pull:} Where push-based systems offer low latency for
	      sporadic events, and pull-based (sampling) systems align better with
	      continuous-time simulation but require careful handling of idle
	      recomputation.
	\item \emph{Sync vs. Async:} Where synchronous execution simplifies
	      reasoning about ordering and consistency (essential for strictly
	      ordered simulations), while asynchronous execution is necessary for
	      decoupling and scalability, albeit at the cost of non-determinism.
\end{itemize}

Crucially, we addressed the inherent challenges of dynamic dependency graphs.
We explored the phenomenon of \emph{glitches} and how they must be managed
through topological sorting in local environments.

Extended to distributed environments, where the lack of a global clock renders
local glitch-freedom techniques insufficient, we categorised the solution space
into three distinct paradigms:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \emph{Strong Consistency}: Classical middleware (like
	      \textsc{DREAM}) which enforces glitch freedom via heavy
	      coordination transactions.
	\item \emph{Eventual Consistency}: Industrial actor frameworks (like
	      \textsc{Akka}) which prioritise availability and scalability by
	      accepting temporary inconsistencies and utilising flow control.
	\item \emph{Deterministic Concurrency}: Emergent models (like
	      \textsc{Lingua Franca}) which leverage \emph{logical time} to
	      restore deterministic execution order without the bottlenecks
	      of centralised locking.
\end{enumerate}

Finally, we surveyed the current landscape of \ac{JVM}-based reactive
frameworks to evaluate their suitability for the objectives of this thesis,
revealing a misalignment with discrete-event simulation's requirements, due to
the prioritisation of throughput and asynchronous handling.

This theoretical toolbox and the analysis of the industry standard solutions,
provides the necessary vocabulary and knowledge to discuss the architectural
decisions of the proposed simulation engine in the following chapters,
specifically regarding how to balance the strict ordering required by \ac{DES}
with the flexible composability offered by reactive programming.
